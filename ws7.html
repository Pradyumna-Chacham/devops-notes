Retrospectives

Glossary 2: a retrospective is "a practice in which team members discuss the causes and consequences of an unexpected operation outage or deployment failure." The README adds the synonyms: a retrospective is also called a postmortem or a hotwash, "a mechanism to reflect and communicate what happened during a DevOps process, especially when trying to learn from past mistakes".

The adage is "Looking Back to Move Forward". Beyond one incident, with a collection of retrospectives "developers can perform a meta-analysis of them to uncover trends and systematic issues with deployment processes".

Why hold them (slide)

  • Build trust
  • Try new things
  • Improve continuously
  • (Self) reflection
  • Collaborative learning
  • Shared understanding

How to run them well (slides)

  • Agree on vocabulary
  • Use ice breakers
  • Foster team psychological safety
  • Make it fun: give it a theme (the slides' example was an outdoor trip)
  • Five quick tips: keep it simple, incorporate novelty, stay focused, make it action-oriented, bring in an outside perspective
Exam anglePsychological safety is the load-bearing idea. If people fear blame they hide what happened, and the team cannot learn. A retrospective asks "what in our process allowed this?", not "who did this?". Contrast this with the siloed world below, where "organizational silos blame each other for production problems".

Terms that mean nearly the same thing

TermFlavor

Workshop 7 itself

Deliverable: a 1 to 4 minute screen-recorded video retrospective of Workshop 5. The rubric was four equal points, which is also a fair outline of what a retrospective records:

Video showsWhy it belongs in a retrospective

The practical skills were screen capture and three editing moves (import, split, export), with the lesson that a retrospective is a communication artifact: short, legible, under the size limit.

A postmortem skeleton worth being able to sketch
Title / date / authors
Summary          one paragraph: what broke, for whom, for how long
Timeline         when it started, was detected, was mitigated, was resolved
Impact           users / requests / revenue affected  (the blast radius)
Root cause       the technical cause AND the process gap that let it through
What went well   detection, rollback, communication that worked
What went wrong  missing tests, alerts, runbooks, slow rollback
Action items     concrete change, owner, due date  (e.g. add a pipeline stage, an alert, a test)

The README pointed to a real example, Anthropic's September 2025 "A postmortem of three recent issues", and asked you to look for how they proposed modifying their DevOps pipeline as a result. That is the pattern to remember: a good postmortem ends in changes to tests, evaluations and monitoring, not in an apology.

Honest takeaways from Workshop 5 that would fit the video: test the playbook on a freshly provisioned VM; make every task idempotent so reruns are safe; keep secrets out of the repo with vars_prompt and .gitignore; let the playbook verify its own result with uri.

DevOps silos and the "wall of confusion"

Silo
A team that works in isolation with its own goals, tools and knowledge. Traditionally (and sometimes today) Dev and Ops are siloed.
Wall of confusion
The barrier between Dev and Ops. Developers want change; operators want stability. Each side lacks the other's context, so releases are "thrown over the wall".

The quote to know (Leite et al., ACM Computing Surveys 2019): in the pre-DevOps era the interface between the two "was usually a ticket system: development teams demanded the deployment of new software versions, and the operations staff manually managed those tickets. Theoretically, this structure provides higher stability for software in production. However, in practice, it also results in long delays between code updates and deployment, as well as ineffective problem-solving processes, in which organizational silos blame each other for production problems."

DevOps seeks to unite the silos. Small cross-functional teams (the two large pizzas rule) each own a service, with automation replacing the ticket queue.

Change ownership: "You are the support person"

Glossary 2: change ownership is "a practice in which developers are responsible for software changes for all phases, including development, testing, deployment, and fixing problems." The summary slide puts it as: "the software developer has responsibility for the consequences of changes."

Quotes from the Finnish case study, shown under the title "DevOps means ownership and responsibility":

  • "It means giving the team more responsibility and control… don't handover to operations your deployment."
  • "DevOps is when you have full responsibility of the software you are developing in production."
  • "Persons who are developing the applications are also the same ones to maintain them in production. If something goes wrong then the same persons are investigating the problems and fixing them."
Because the developer owns the change……they care about
They get paged when it failsTests, monitoring, telemetry, safe rollouts
They do the deploymentAutomating it (pipelines, playbooks)
They fix the problemSmall changes, fast rollback, feature flags
They explain what happenedHonest retrospectives

The 2015 survey behind the adages (17 teams from nine companies) asked how often teams used eleven continuous-deployment practices: automated deployment, automated unit testing, automated system testing, code review, dark launching, end-user communication, feature flags, telemetry, branching, change ownership, staging. Recognise that list as "CD practices".

Change management

The study guide lists change management as its own bullet. Pull the threads together: every change to production is a risk, so DevOps controls change by making each one small (CI, units of change), reviewed (pull requests at diff time), automatically verified (pipeline stages and quality gates), gradually exposed (flags, canaries, rings), owned (change ownership), reversible (rollback plan) and learned from (retrospectives). The pre-DevOps version of change management was the ticket queue and the gatekeeping ops team.

"Comfort the customer with discomfort"

Slide title: releases are part of the user experience, with the question "does the user choose… or not?". Continuous deployment means the product changes under the user constantly. Some users welcome that; others are disturbed when a feature appears, moves or disappears without warning. Teams have to manage that discomfort: communicate with end users, let them opt in to previews (early-adopter rings), roll out gradually, and keep the ability to turn things back. The slides paired it with a paper on availability and accountability for IoT updates, where an update the user did not choose has physical consequences.

All ten adages

Two other quotations used as slide openers: "There are only two hard things in Computer Science: cache invalidation and naming things" (Phil Karlton), and Lamport's line that a distributed system is one where "the failure of a computer you didn't even know existed can render your own computer unusable".

Exam format, as announced

  • Paper exam, in class, pen preferred. Assigned seats. Phones in a bag; no smart watches; no calculator needed.
  • Concepts, not exact docker / ansible / shell syntax. Question types: true / false with corrections, short answer, questions about code or scripts, and matching concepts.
  • True / false with corrections: if false, underline the word(s) you want to change and write the new word(s) below. No simple negation: turning "is made of fresh water" into "is not made of fresh water" earns no points. Replace the wrong word with the right one ("fresh" → "salt").
  • Matching concepts: draw lines from items on the left to the most related item on the right. Left items may connect to zero, one or several items, so do not force every item to match.
  • Not on the midterm even though on slides: DevOps job postings, semantically rich identifiers, unikernels, reverse proxy, docker command syntax.

Answer in your head first, then open each one. On the real T/F questions you underline the wrong word and write the fix. A plain "not" earns nothing.
True / FalseThe three main goals of a retrospective are who caused it, how to punish it, and how to hide it.
False. The goals are what worked, what didn't work, and what can we do better. Blame destroys the psychological safety a retrospective depends on.
True / False"Change ownership" means the operations team is responsible for the consequences of software changes.
False. Change operations team to software developer.
True / FalseIn the pre-DevOps era, the interface between development and operations was usually a ticket system.
True. Which led to long delays and silos blaming each other.
True / FalseThe "wall of confusion" describes the barrier between a container and its host filesystem.
False. It is the barrier between development and operations teams.
True / FalseTelemetry is a practice in which code is instrumented to compute metrics about feature use, performance and stability.
True. Glossary 2.
True / FalseMicroservices are created as large, stateful instances connected through a shared database.
False. They are small and often stateless instances connected through a central discovery service and property store.
True / FalseThe adage "Looking back to move forward" is most closely tied to feature flags.
False. Change feature flags to retrospectives.
True / False"Be fast to deploy but slow to release" is supported by dark launching.
True. Code is in production (deployed) but invisible to users (not released).
True / FalseContinuous deployment is a process in which software changes are manually tested and deployed to production once per quarter.
False. Changes are incremental and are automatically tested, vetted and deployed.
True / FalseA hotwash is a kind of feature flag.
False. A hotwash is a kind of retrospective (an immediate after-action review).

Short answerExplain how DevOps addresses the wall of confusion. Mention at least three practices.
It removes the hand-off. One small team owns a service from code to production (change ownership, two-pizza teams); automation replaces tickets (CI/CD pipelines, configuration as code); shared telemetry and blameless retrospectives replace blame; gradual releases with fast rollback make frequent change safe enough that ops no longer needs to resist it.
Short answerA deployment caused a 40-minute outage. Outline the retrospective you would run.
Gather the timeline and data first. Set a blameless tone (psychological safety). Ask what worked (e.g. alert fired, rollback was fast), what didn't (e.g. no canary, missing test), and what to do better. End with specific action items with owners, such as adding a staging test, a ring-0 canary, or an alert, and track them. Keep the write-up so trends across incidents can be analyzed.
Short answerTrace one code change from a developer's laptop to all users, naming a concept from each workshop.
The developer works in a container (WS1) on a feature branch and opens a pull request, the diff-time review point (WS2). The PR event triggers a GitHub Actions workflow that tests it (WS3). After merge, an idempotent Ansible playbook configures the servers (WS4) and deploys the app with the new feature behind a flag, dark (WS5). It is enabled ring by ring, canaries first, while telemetry is watched and the blast radius stays small (WS6). If anything goes wrong it is rolled back and the team holds a retrospective (WS7).
Short answerWhy is "no simple negation" a rule on the true / false questions, and how do you correct "Ansible requires agents on managed nodes"?
Adding "not" shows you know the statement is false but not what is true. Replace the wrong term: underline "requires agents" and write "requires no agents (is agentless), connecting over SSH".
