From the 3-tier architecture to modern infrastructure

The lecture reviews the 3-tier (layered) architecture, shows where it breaks, and then adds one infrastructure component per desirable property until the system survives load, failures and outages.

When it is not enough

The slide gives three failure pictures. Each one is a single point of failure or a capacity limit:

Slide pictureWhat goes wrongProperty that is missing
A huge crowd opening the login pageAll users arrive at once and one app server cannot serve themScalable, available
An unplugged power cordThe app server is unplugged or its VM is deleted, so the service is goneAvailable, robust
A bucket full to the brimDisks are full and incoming data requests exceed capacityScalable, durable, regulation

Slide 7, "Towards Architecture 2.0": traditional architectural styles are insufficient for modern web-scale workloads. Moving forward there are two options: patch architectures (keep the 3 tiers and add components around them) or novel architectures (the microservices style at the end of the lecture). The slide's pictures are a patched road and the Amazon.com site.

Slides 4 and 8 are reference diagrams: the Microsoft Cloud Design Patterns poster, and a set of real cloud architectures (a mobile game backend, Drupal hosting). Their message is that real systems are made of many of these patterns.

Desirable properties of modern infrastructure

Eight properties, in the slide's order and wording. This list is the spine of the lecture, and each component later in the deck is introduced by the properties it ensures.

1. Availableslide wording
No or limited interruption to provided services.
2. Scalableslide wording
Can increase specific units in response to demand.
3. Efficientslide wording
Avoid redundant work, shift responsibility to low-cost components.
4. Robustslide wording
Graceful response to failure of infrastructure or services.
5. Durableslide wording
Operations and data persistent across partitions.
6. Regulationslide wording
Can throttle incoming work into a component.
7. Isolationslide wording
Can reduce the impact of one component's behaviour on others.
8. Resilientslide wording
Can adapt to unknown and unexpected situations (what happens if a region goes down, or if Google kills GCP?).
Do not confuseRobust is a graceful response to known kinds of failure (a server dies). Resilient is adapting to the unknown and unexpected (a whole region, or a vendor, disappears). Available is about uninterrupted service; durable is about data and operations surviving.

The rest of the lecture grows one example site, www.devops.com on AWS, one step at a time. The AWS product names are examples; the concepts are what matter.

Making it available and scalable

DNS services (slide 16)

  • A simple routing service. It turns a request to www.devops.com into an IP address, projected into the AWS infrastructure (Amazon Route 53, with a "hosted zone").
  • Routing options: latency-based routing, geo DNS, weighted round robin.
  • Simple round robin: several names such as www0.devops.com, www1.devops.com, and so on, taken in turn.

Load balancing (slide 17)

  • Dynamic balancing and scaling.
  • Ensure only healthy instances receive requests.
  • Not only can it route a request, it can request a new auto-scaling instance if needed.
  • Conditions that trigger scaling, as listed: latency greater than 4 seconds; CPU utilization greater than 70%; request count; count of 5xx responses.

What happens when the load balancer goes down? (slide 18)

The load balancer is itself a single point of failure. The answer is leader election: stand-by servers can take over in the event the leader goes down. The slide's meme is two identical Spider-Men; the diagram shows clients and servers with one active and the others on stand-by.

Ensuring efficiency and regulation

Content delivery network (slide 21)

  • Deliver frequently accessed content from the edge (Amazon CloudFront in front of an S3 bucket, on its own name media.devops.com).
  • Don't waste CPU, sockets and worker threads on static requests.
  • "The most common DDoS attack is to request a large static file, e.g. a 100 MB PDF file." Serving it from a CDN protects the servers.
  • Example content: JavaScript libraries and CSS; images and videos; popular objects.

Caches and queues (slide 22)

  • Cache popular dynamic requests. Data is in memory, not on disk. Providers: Memcached, Redis (an "ElastiCache" tier).
  • Example content: the front page of Reddit; computationally intensive requests such as driving directions from Raleigh to Florida during Spring Break.
  • Queues: data waiting to be processed and stored by the back end. A queue absorbs bursts so the back end is not overrun (this is regulation).

Circuit breakers and bulkheads (slide 23)

Circuit breaker. "Eliminate connections to faulty services." (Martin Fowler's pattern.) Stop calling a service that keeps failing so callers do not pile up waiting on it.

Bulkheads. "Effectively isolate components and protect from cascading failures through the enforcement of limits (load shedding)." Named after the watertight compartments in a ship's hull. Example on the slide: no matter how many requests are applied, NGINX has a hard limit on the number of processes it will create.

The slide's source text names only the idea. The "open after repeated failures, fail fast" behaviour is the standard description of the pattern, so treat that detail as general knowledge rather than slide wording.

Ensuring isolation

Availability zones (slide 25)

  • Isolated production environment. Create pools or zones of your production environment that are isolated from other instances.
  • Use cases: switch requests when deploying a new version; have environments resilient to pollution (a corrupted cache); avoid slow spin-up by anticipating a large traffic boost (lunch or prime-time hours).
  • The sidebar picture is "cascading failures: the pyramid check", a stack of glasses where one falling glass brings down the rest.

Regions (slide 26)

  • Geographically placed production environments, also isolated from each other.
  • Use cases: serve traffic closest to where it occurs (Asia, Europe, South America); handle different laws and content-distribution rules.
Availability zoneRegion
What it isAn isolated pool inside one regionA geographic placement of a whole production environment

Microservices

The "novel architecture" option. Split services into single deployable units.

  • Scalability: can run as many instances of particular services as needed across multiple servers.
  • Robustness and isolation: can still run other services if one fails.

This is the same idea as the lecture-2 and Glossary 2 definition of microservices: small, often stateless instances connected through a central discovery service and property store.

Case study: Netflix (slides 32 and 33)

PieceWhat the slides say

What AWS doesDiscovery, load balancing, recommendations, profiles, and 1 billion metrics streaming to Atlas

The lesson: services are stateless so any instance can answer; the state lives in a replicated data store. That is what lets each part be scaled, replaced or lost independently.

Platform and vendor risks (slide 34)

Depending on a platform is itself a risk. Two examples from the slide:

  • Heroku "kills free hosting tier, replacing it with a new service that can only run 18 hours per day" (2015).
  • Linode assigned maintenance windows in which a reboot will occur; the assigned windows are not changeable.

This is the resilient property again: what do you do if a provider changes terms or disappears? Answers the deck points to are regions, multiple zones, and avoiding lock-in.

Definitions from this deck

3-tier architecture
Web / presentation layer, application layer, database layer. Often sufficient for many applications.
Patch architectureslide wording
Keep the traditional layered style and add infrastructure components around it.
Novel architectureslide wording
A different style built for web scale, such as microservices.
Single point of failure
One component whose loss stops the whole system (one app server, one load balancer, one database).
Load balancer
Sends traffic to healthy instances and can request new ones. Ensures availability and scalability.
Auto-scaling group
A set of instances that grows or shrinks on a trigger such as CPU above 70% or latency above 4 seconds.
Leader election
A stand-by server takes over when the active (leader) one goes down.
DNS
Turns a name such as www.devops.com into an IP address. Routing options: latency-based, geo, weighted round robin, simple round robin.
CDNcontent delivery network
Delivers static content from a third-party provider's edge servers. Ensures availability and efficiency.
Cache
Fast in-memory storage (Memcached, Redis) for popular dynamic results.
Queue
Data waiting to be processed and stored by the back end.
Circuit breaker
Eliminate connections to faulty services.
Bulkhead
Isolates components and protects from cascading failures through enforced limits (load shedding).
Load shedding
Refusing excess work once a limit is reached so the rest keeps working.
Cascading failure
One failing component causing the components that depend on it to fail in turn.
Availability zone
An isolated pool of production instances inside a region. Spreads risk.
Region
A geographically placed production environment, for latency and to meet local laws.
Mirroringreplication
Keeping a copy of the data on a standby server. Ensures durability.
Sharding
Splitting data across servers by a key. Ensures scalability and isolation.
Monolith
One application, one executable, all services together.
Microservicesslide wording
Split services into single deployable units, each run in its own executable.
Stateless service
Keeps no client state in the instance, so any instance can answer. State lives in a replicated store.
Open Connect
Netflix's own CDN: boxes co-located with ISPs that serve all video data.
Vendor risk
The risk that a provider changes or removes its service (Heroku free tier, Linode forced reboots).

Practice

Not on the midterm. This lecture came after the exam (Oct 7), so it is for the final and the project. The questions use the same formats.

Answer in your head first, then open each one. On true / false, underline the wrong word and write the fix; a plain "not" earns nothing.
True / FalseA load balancer sends requests to all instances, including ones that have failed.
False. Underline all instances, including ones that have failed → only healthy instances.
True / FalseMirroring splits data across servers so each holds a part of it.
False. Mirroring → Sharding. Mirroring keeps a copy (durability).
True / FalseA cache stores popular dynamic requests on disk.
False. on disk → in memory (Memcached, Redis).
True / FalseA CDN is used to deliver static content such as images and JavaScript libraries.
True. It shifts delivery from your infrastructure to a third-party provider.
True / FalseAvailability zones are geographically placed environments that serve users closest to where they are.
False. Availability zones → Regions. Zones are isolated pools inside a region.
True / FalseBulkheads protect against cascading failures by enforcing limits.
True. Load shedding.
True / FalseMicroservices run all services in a single executable.
False. a single executable → their own executable each (that describes a monolith).
True / FalseThe ability to adapt to unknown and unexpected situations, such as a region going down, is called robustness.
False. robustness → resilience. Robust is graceful response to failure.
True / FalseAt Netflix, AWS serves the video data.
False. AWS handles no video data; the Open Connect CDN serves it.

True. Slide 18.
MatchingMatch each component to the property it mainly ensures. Components: load balancer · CDN · availability zone · mirroring · sharding · bulkhead · cache. Properties: availability and scalability · efficiency · isolation · durability · scalability and isolation · protection from cascading failure.
Load balancer ↔ availability and scalability. CDN ↔ efficiency (and availability). Availability zone ↔ isolation. Mirroring ↔ durability. Sharding ↔ scalability and isolation. Bulkhead ↔ protection from cascading failure. Cache ↔ efficiency.
MatchingName the infrastructure piece: (a) turns a name into an IP address (b) skip unhealthy servers (c) serve a 100 MB PDF without using your CPU (d) stand-by takes over (e) stop calling a failing service.
(a) DNS. (b) Load balancer. (c) CDN. (d) Leader election. (e) Circuit breaker.
Self-checkHow are availability, isolation, regulation and scalability related to DevOps? (slide 28)
Availability and scalability are what DevOps pipelines deliver safely: a change must not interrupt service, and the system must grow with load. Isolation (zones, regions, bulkheads) keeps a bad change or failure small, which is the blast-radius idea behind rings and canaries, and gives you a place to switch traffic for a deployment or rollback. Regulation (throttling, queues, limits) protects services from overload while changes roll out. DevOps adds the automation, monitoring and fast recovery that keep these properties true as the code keeps changing.
Self-checkWhy is the 3-tier architecture sometimes not enough?
It has single points of failure and fixed capacity: an unplugged app server or deleted VM takes the service down, a crowd of users overwhelms one server, and full disks or too many data requests overload one database.
Self-checkWhat does a stateless service buy you?
Any instance can answer any request, so instances can be added, removed, restarted or rolled back without losing user state. The state is kept in a replicated store instead.
